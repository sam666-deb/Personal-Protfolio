Melbourne doesn't market itself as a quantum computing city the way it markets coffee or AFL, but spend enough time around its universities and startups and you'll notice it: researchers at the University of Melbourne and Monash working on quantum hardware, RMIT and Swinburne pushing human-computer interaction (HCI) research, and a growing cluster of startups quietly experimenting with what quantum-accelerated machine learning could mean for products people use every day. As someone studying IT and systems in Tasmania and building software just across the strait, this shift is hard to ignore — especially where it intersects with a field I care about a lot: HCI.

The specific intersection I want to talk about is one that doesn't have a settled name yet, but I'll use the term **quantum personalisation and profiling** — using quantum or quantum-inspired computing to process behavioural data at a scale and speed classical systems struggle with, in order to build sharper, faster, more predictive models of individual users.

## What "quantum personalisation" actually means

Strip away the buzzword and the idea is simple: personalisation engines — the systems behind your For You page, your ad targeting, your "recommended for you" playlist — are fundamentally optimisation and pattern-matching problems over huge, high-dimensional datasets. Quantum computing (and quantum-inspired classical algorithms already in production today) is particularly good at exactly that kind of problem: searching enormous solution spaces and finding correlations that classical systems would take much longer to surface.

Applied to personalisation, that means:

- Profiling that updates in near real-time as behaviour shifts, rather than in daily or hourly batches
- Modelling far more variables per person — not just what you clicked, but sequencing, timing, hesitation patterns, cross-platform behaviour — combined in ways that are computationally expensive today
- Recommendation and targeting systems that generalise from smaller amounts of data per person, because the underlying pattern-matching is more powerful

None of this requires a fault-tolerant quantum computer sitting in a Melbourne data centre tomorrow. Quantum-inspired algorithms running on classical hardware are already edging in this direction, and they're the more immediate, practical version of this story.

## Where HCI comes in

This is where my own interest sharpens. HCI is the discipline that asks: given that a system *can* do something, how does a human actually experience it, and what does that interaction do to them over time? A more powerful personalisation engine isn't just a backend upgrade — it changes the interaction itself. Interfaces get better at holding attention, better at knowing exactly which notification, colour, or nudge will produce a reaction, and better at doing this consistently across millions of people simultaneously.

That's a genuinely double-edged capability, and Melbourne's research community sits close to both edges of it.

## The upside

I don't think this is a story with only villains. Some real, tangible benefits:

- **Education**: adaptive learning platforms that respond to how a specific student actually learns — not a demographic average — could meaningfully help students who are underserved by one-size-fits-all curricula.
- **Healthcare and mental health support**: personalisation applied to early intervention (flagging patterns associated with declining mental health, tailoring support resources to an individual) has real potential, particularly relevant for a country grappling with youth mental health strain.
- **Accessibility**: interfaces that adapt in real time to a user's specific cognitive or physical needs, rather than offering static accessibility settings, is a genuinely exciting HCI direction.
- **Immersive and AR/VR experiences**: personalisation that adapts a virtual environment to how a specific user perceives and interacts with it — closer to my own AR/VR background — could make immersive training and education tools significantly more effective.

## The cost, especially for youth

The same underlying capability that makes an adaptive tutor possible also makes a more addictive short-form video feed possible, and there's no clean technical line separating the two.

For younger users specifically, the concerns compound:

- **Engagement optimisation becomes harder to resist.** A recommendation system that already knows what keeps an adult scrolling is powerful; one modelling a still-developing brain's impulse control and social-comparison triggers with greater precision is a different order of concern.
- **Profiling this detailed is hard to meaningfully consent to.** "Accept cookies" was already a fiction for most adults. A profiling system operating on subtler behavioural signals, updating faster than a user can notice or object to, makes informed consent close to meaningless — and most privacy literacy education targets exactly the *slower*, more visible version of this problem.
- **Filter bubbles get more precise, not just bigger.** More accurate modelling doesn't necessarily mean more accurate *information* — it can mean a more efficiently narrowed worldview, which is a particular risk during the years someone is still forming their political, social, and cultural identity.
- **The power asymmetry widens.** A handful of organisations with quantum or quantum-inspired infrastructure gain a capability most users, parents, schools, and even regulators can't inspect, audit, or meaningfully push back on.

## Where I land on this

I don't think the answer is to stop building — Melbourne's research strength here is a genuine asset, and I'd rather see this capability developed with careful HCI input than developed elsewhere without it. But I do think it raises the bar for what "responsible design" needs to mean once profiling gets this powerful: transparency about what's being modelled, real (not theatrical) opt-outs, age-appropriate defaults that don't rely on a teenager correctly guessing what a system is trying to do to them, and researchers in the room who are explicitly asking "what does this do to the person on the other end of the interaction" rather than only "does this improve engagement metrics."

That's the version of HCI research I want to be part of — the kind that treats a more capable system as a reason to ask harder questions, not fewer. If you're working in this space in Melbourne, or thinking about it from a policy, research, or design angle, I'd genuinely like to compare notes.
